# Split the owner into attention and memory

No single established model covers all four behaviours the simulated owner needs, but a composite of well-studied pieces does, and every piece can run as a seeded draw in the harness instead of as a judgment inside the owner model. The recommended owner multiplies two factors. An **attention factor**, owned by the policy, decides whether the owner reads a passage: **a(w) = 0.18 + 0.82 / (1 + w/500)**, where w is the count of new words before that passage in the stop. Its shape comes from code-review size experiments and its constants from prose-reading data, with the central value a judgment call. A **memory factor**, owned by the item, decides whether a passage the owner has read brings back a requirement they never stated. It is a logistic in the ACT-R form, with cells anchored on prospective-memory and cued-recall rates: **0.25 for an adjacent cue and 0.75 for a direct cue at medium salience**, running from 0.10 to 0.90 across three salience tiers, with the odds divided by the number of unsaid items the same passage touches. Omission-neglect research supports the existing rule that silence is not a contradiction: unprompted reviewers notice roughly **15–35% of omissions**. Requirements-volatility data say scripted shifts should be mostly additions and clarifications, not reversals. The placeholder curve 1/(1 + w/1,500) has the right shape and the right constant for code review. But the owner reads prose, which people skim far harder than code, and no reader catches every problem in even a short text, so its intercept of 1 is too generous. The three policies then change only attention: diligent reads everything, bounded reads by the curve, rubber-stamp reads nothing, and memory stays fallible under all three. Without a person playing the owner, validation rests on three things: replaying real human transcripts, checking the owner model's per-event judgments against human labels, and showing the arm comparison holds across the plausible range of every constant. Calibrating on one person would mostly measure that person: in one elicitation experiment, which person played the client explained **18–26%** of the outcome. The largest uncertainty is transfer. No study measures how often real product owners recall unsaid requirements when shown a plan, so every constant beyond the curve shapes is borrowed from neighbouring tasks.

Each citation carries a label for how closely the research team read it: **full text** (read directly), **full text, tool-assisted** (full text read through an extraction or summarising pass, numbers not all re-checked by hand), **partial** (selected sections of the full text), **abstract**, **secondary** (another author's summary of the original), **snippet** (search summary only), and **vendor** (material from a company selling the product studied).

## The composite rule fits in one harness function

The owner model call already classifies each stop, answers direct questions, and lists contradictions tied to oracle items. The composite needs three additions to its structured output. For each contradiction, it quotes the passage. For each askable item not yet disclosed, it gives a touch level (none, adjacent, or direct), quotes the passage, and drafts a one-line recall in the owner's voice. For each direct question, it marks whether the question is targeted (it names the item's topic) or broad ("any other edge cases?"). The harness finds each quoted passage in the stop's new text, counts the new words that come before it in presentation order (final message, then plan, then named prose files), computes the attention factor, and draws. Each kept contradiction and each kept recall line goes into the reply. A recalled item becomes disclosed and enters the answer record, exactly as if the agent had asked about it.

The draws use one seeded uniform per item per new cue, shared across the three policies. Because the bounded probability never exceeds the diligent one, an item the bounded owner catches is always caught by the diligent owner too, so policy differences within a seed reflect the policies and not draw noise. A failed draw is not repeated on the same text. A new draw happens only when new text touches the item. That follows the conditionalisation rule in Raaijmakers and Shiffrin's SAM model: a fresh recovery chance arises only when the probe contains a new cue, because repeated attempts with the same cue "are based on essentially the same information" ([Raaijmakers & Shiffrin 1981](https://raaijmakers.edu.fmg.uva.nl/PDFs/Raaijmakers%20and%20Shiffrin%201981.pdf), partial). Without this rule, an arm could push recall toward certainty by re-showing the same plan. The design already shows a re-read file as its changes only, which delivers most of this for free.

Setting f = 0, q = 1 and k = 1,500, and measuring w cumulatively across the change, reproduces the pilot's placeholder exactly. The pilot therefore sits at the optimistic corner of the recommended sweep, and its results stay comparable.

|---|---|---|---|---|

Diligent keeps fallible memory on purpose. A diligent owner who recalled every unsaid item a document touched would hand the oracle to whichever arm shows the most text. The design's rule that "a correction corrects; it doesn't add" exists to prevent exactly that. Under the composite, a drawn recall line becomes the one licensed kind of addition. The trimming call must keep drawn recall lines, and the leak judge must be told which lines were drawn, so it does not count them as leaks. Diligent's q = 1 stays an idealised bound, a perfect proofreader, not an estimate of any human. The bounded policy carries the human estimate.

## Owners fail to notice silence unless something supplies the expectation

The first behaviour, an owner who hides nothing yet fails to realise what they left unsaid, matches a well-replicated finding in consumer judgment called omission neglect. In one experiment, neutral-mood participants rated a restaurant described by 5 of 13 attributes **no lower than one described by all 13** (purchase interest 5.5 versus 5.2) ([Frontiers in Psychology 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9680845/), partial). Sanbonmatsu and colleagues trace the effect to people overweighting the attributes they were given. They found three things that reduce it: domain knowledge, considering one's criteria before judging, and comparison with an alternative described on other attributes ([Sanbonmatsu et al. 2003](https://katalog.lib.cas.cz/KNAV/EdsRecord/edsjsr,edsjsr.1480218), abstract). Two moderators bear directly on a ten-change project. After a delay, people give missing attributes **30–41% less weight** and feel more confident across three studies ([Hernandez et al. 2023](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/4AC0063B7F3EB5730D628D97FA7CDD76/core-reader), partial; the percentages are this report's calculation from the published means). And the more diagnostic the presented information looks, the less sensitive people are to what it leaves out ([Hernandez, Han & Kardes 2014](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jbrese/v67y2014i5p874-881.html), abstract).

Reading research shows the same thing at the word level. Asked after each test sentence whether anything was wrong, readers noticed an **omitted "the" 67% of the time and a repeated "the" only 46%**, against 86% for transposed words and 90% for a repeated noun. On trials where the error went unnoticed, eye movements were statistically indistinguishable from reading a correct sentence ([Huang & Staub 2021](https://propel.umass.edu/sites/default/files/publications/2025-03/HuangStaubLLC2021.pdf), full text). The noisy-channel account in that review explains why: when the reader's model predicts an item, the reader fills it in. An owner's background assumption is the omitted "the" of a plan. It is the least likely to be missed precisely because the owner expects it to be there.

Requirements engineering supplies the owner-side version. Ferrari and colleagues define tacit knowledge as knowledge a customer can access but does not pass on. When an analyst followed up on an ambiguous statement, the follow-up exposed hidden knowledge in **91–96% of cases**. For software-engineer customers, 59% of what surfaced was requirement-level knowledge the customer had "incorrectly assumed … as understood" ([Ferrari, Spoletini & Gnesi 2016](https://openportal.isti.cnr.it/data/2016/353983/2016_353983.postprint.pdf), full text). Aranda and colleagues separated holding knowledge from reporting it. Analysts who heard a complete domain description put **35% of its elements in a free written report but 71% in a cued questionnaire** in the complex domain (56% versus 67% in the simple one) ([Aranda et al., visibility study](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.13691), full text). People hold far more than they spontaneously produce, and what they produce depends on the cue.

Review studies show how rarely an unprompted reader catches a silence. Ad hoc requirements reviewers found a median of **1 in 5 missing-or-ambiguous functionality faults**, checklist reviewers 0, and reviewers given a scenario aimed at missing functionality 2 ([Porter, Votta & Basili 1995](https://drum.lib.umd.edu/items/96204769-6905-4196-b903-709ad81148ee/full), full text; the class merges missing with ambiguous). Novice security reviewers found **23% of seeded omissions with only a high-level reference list, but 92% when the procedure made them compare the stories against that list item by item** ([Villamizar et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2009.03678), full text). Omissions also dominate what escapes review. Omitted logic was the largest category of persistent production errors in Glass's study, at **30% of 200** ([Glass 1981](https://www.ida.liu.se/~TDDD80/seminarier/Persistent_Software_Errors.pdf), full text, OCR). An often-repeated claim that Glass found three in four bugs were omissions does not match his table. Nor did the research find controlled evidence that omissions are detected at some fixed fraction of the rate of wrong content: individual ad hoc detection rates were similarly low for both classes. The robust finding is narrower. An omission produces no signal, so a reader catches it only by comparing against an expectation, and every effective intervention in this literature supplies that expectation from outside the reader's memory: cueing, comparison lists, scenarios, traceability matrices, functional checks.

Four simulator rules follow. First, the owner states their visible items and holds the rest without auditing themselves, which supports "never volunteers". Second, silence about a held item is not a contradiction, which supports the trimming rule. Recall on review is the controlled channel for the 15–35% of silences a real owner does catch. Third, askable and recognised-on-sight items differ in kind. An askable item can be put into words, so a cue can call it back. A recognised-on-sight item, by the design's own definition, cannot be put into words, and its absence produces no signal. So it keeps its contradiction-only trigger, and recall on review applies to askable items only. A sensitivity variant can admit recognised-on-sight items at direct cues, one salience tier down. Fourth, targeted and broad questions deserve different treatment. A question that names the topic is a direct cue, and Ferrari's 91–96% yield supports keeping the answer every time. A broad "anything else?" is the generic-checklist case, which did no better than ad hoc reading in Porter's data, and Pitts and Browne found specific procedural prompts beat general interrogatory ones ([via Aranda et al., experience study](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.12538), secondary). Recalling each in-scope askable item at its adjacent-cue rate fits that evidence better than the current rule, which hands over every askable item about the change.

All of this belongs in the harness, not the prompt. When the owner model holds the full truth and is merely told to be reticent, it leaks. In HumanEvalComm, the simulated user recovered missing information for **32.5–40.7% of non-question responses** from open-source models ([Wu & Fard, HumanEvalComm](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.00215), full text, tool-assisted). In tau2-bench, coupling the simulated user to environment state cut conversations with simulator errors to **16%, against 40–47%** in prompt-only domains ([Barres et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.07982), full text, tool-assisted).

## Direct cues bring back unsaid items three times as often as adjacent ones

ACT-R's declarative memory gives the most practical rule for recall on review. The probability of retrieving an item is **P = 1 / (1 + e^(−(A − τ)/s))**. Activation A adds a base level, which tracks how often and how recently the item has been used, to spreading activation from whatever is currently in view. Each cue term contributes S − ln(fan), so a cue linked to many items pushes each of them less. The noise is logistic with scale s ([ACT-R tutorial notes, units 4](https://huxianyin.github.io/blog/2020/11/09/tutorialUnit4) and [5](https://huxianyin.github.io/blog/2020/11/14/tutorialUnit5), secondary). The base level's power-law decay d = 0.5 is the one near-universal constant. The threshold and noise are fitted per task. Fitting vocabulary learning over 1-day and 7-day gaps, Pavlik and Anderson obtained **s = 0.252 and τ = −0.669** (r² = .971) ([Pavlik & Anderson 2003](https://www.gwern.net/docs/www/act-r.psy.cmu.edu/f4b85d71f1bd93309c094119607097a071bb09e3.pdf), full text), and tutorial models use s between 0.15 and 0.5. The harness's seeded draw is ACT-R's noise term. Drawing u from a uniform and recalling when u < P is mathematically identical to adding logistic noise to activation and recalling when it clears the threshold. The official ACT-R site was unreachable during the research, so stock defaults come from a primary modelling paper plus a student's tutorial notes.

The rivals fit worse. SAM's sampling rule (an item's cue strength divided by the summed strengths of all items linked to that cue) captures cue overload neatly. But computing it for one item needs strengths to every competitor and a simulated search with stopping parameters, and the 1981 model has no forgetting over days ([Raaijmakers & Shiffrin 1981](https://raaijmakers.edu.fmg.uva.nl/PDFs/Raaijmakers%20and%20Shiffrin%201981.pdf), partial). REM needs feature vectors that no natural-language plan supplies. The Generative Agents memory score (recency + importance + relevance) is a ranking, not a probability. Its exponential recency decay of 0.995 per hour leaves about 3% after 30 days ([Park et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.03442v2), full text, tool-assisted). Across 210 retention data sets, logarithmic and power functions fit best and the simple exponential fit worse ([Rubin & Wenzel 1996](https://cenl.ucsd.edu/Jclub/Rubin_Wenzel_1996.pdf), full text).

ACT-R supplies the form. Its absolute values have to come from human rates, and prospective memory gives the closest behavioural analogue: noticing that something in view bears on an intention not currently in mind. Cues that are themselves the thing being processed (focal cues) trigger spontaneous retrieval. Cues that require noticing an unattended feature (non-focal cues) need monitoring and fail far more often. With four targets during a lexical-decision task, focal cues were caught **.88 and non-focal .75**. With a single target after 500 ongoing trials of dormancy, the figures were **.73 against .18** ([Scullin et al. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2864946), full text, tool-assisted). An owner is never monitoring for a requirement they did not know they had left out, so the dormant condition is the relevant one. The calibration therefore pins two cells. A medium-salience item with a direct cue recalls at **0.75**, inside the dormant-focal .73–.88 band and near Aranda's 67–71% cued capture. The same item with an adjacent cue recalls at **0.25**, between the dormant non-focal .18 and the 15–35% unprompted omission-detection band. Salience tiers then sit 1.1 logits apart:

| Salience tier | Adjacent cue | Direct cue | Direct, passage touches 2 unsaid items | Direct, touches 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Low | .10 | .50 | .33 | .25 |
| Medium | .25 | .75 | .60 | .50 |
| High | .50 | .90 | .82 | .75 |

This table is exactly an ACT-R logistic with source weight W = 1, associative strength S = 1.5 (the tutorial fan model used 1.6), noise s ≈ 0.34 (inside the fitted and tutorial ranges), overlap 0.5 for adjacent and 1 for direct, and tiers 0.37 activation units apart. The high-direct cell of 0.90 sits at the top of the focal band and near Ferrari's 91–96% follow-up yield. Moving from an adjacent to a direct cue multiplies recall by 5 for low-salience items and by 1.8 for high ones. That matches Aranda's larger cued-recall gain in the complex domain (2× against 1.2×) and Scullin's finding that focal cues survive the dormancy that sinks non-focal ones.

The research notes also sketched a version that maps salience to 1, 5 or 20 prior encounters. Over-week gaps, that version produces cells near 0 and 1 that no data support, and a recall rate near 0.5 with no cue at all for high-salience items, which by definition went unsaid. The anchored table avoids both problems, and the no-cue column is zero by rule anyway, because the owner never volunteers.

Cue overload is real in direction and uncertain in size. Nairne argues that what predicts recall is how diagnostic a cue is: its overlap with the target relative to its overlap with competitors. Raising raw overlap "can improve, have no effect, or even decrease retention" ([Nairne 2002](https://scilit.com/publications/d081945c21906d5876e19257ab1c1cf3), abstract). The composite divides the odds by f, the number of eligible unsaid items the same passage touches, which is the soft form of SAM's ratio rule. Strict ACT-R at s ≈ 0.34 would cut a direct medium cue touching three items from .75 to about .11. No cited data support a penalty that size, and it would partly double-count the length penalty already in the attention factor. So λ = 1 is the central value, with 0 and 3 as bounds.

The table is stationary across the trajectory, for four reasons. An unsaid item has no trace inside the trajectory to decay from. The owner's daily work keeps refreshing their own domain knowledge outside it. No source gives forgetting constants for self-relevant requirements as opposed to word lists. And ACT-R's time term would add a unit choice and a calibration with nothing to anchor them. Delay still matters for omissions, though. Hernandez's 30–41% drop and Scullin's dormant non-focal collapse both point that way, while dormant focal cues held up. So a sensitivity variant multiplies adjacent-cue odds by 0.6 after the build and leaves direct cues alone. That turns the medium-adjacent cell into 0.17, close to Scullin's .18.

Take an askable item from a bakery's ordering service: a cancelled order frees its oven slot. The owner never stated it. At change 3, an arm's plan includes a section on how each day's oven capacity is computed. That section describes the very behaviour the item governs without contradicting it, so the touch is direct. Raters had put the item in the medium tier, and no other unsaid item appears in that section, so f = 1 and r = 0.75. The section starts 900 new words into the stop, so under the bounded policy a = 0.18 + 0.82 / (1 + 900/500) ≈ 0.47, and the event probability is 0.47 × 0.75 ≈ 0.35. The diligent owner recalls with probability 0.75, the rubber-stamp owner never. If the draw succeeds, the reply carries the drafted line, "A cancelled order should free its oven slot", and the item is disclosed from then on. If it fails, that section is not drawn on again. A section that only mentioned cancellations in the order flow would have been an adjacent touch, recalled at 0.25 before attention.

## Owners skim prose far harder than reviewers read code

The best controlled evidence on review size comes from Baum, Schneider and Bacchelli, who had 50 mostly professional developers review seeded changes. One defect type, swapped parameters in a method call, was found in **76% of reviews of a 31-line change and 36% when it sat in a 490-line change**. Average effectiveness across all seeded defects was 59% against 35%. Review time grew only about threefold while size grew nearly twelvefold, so attention per line fell ([Baum et al. 2019](https://zenodo.org/records/2001923/files/article.pdf), full text). A hyperbola p = p_max / (1 + w/k) through the swap-defect points gives **p_max ≈ 0.82 and k ≈ 1,530 words**. That uses roughly 4 whitespace words per line of code, the research team's own measurement over local repositories, not a published constant. Rate studies point the same way. Reviewing at no more than 200 lines per hour found about two-thirds of design defects and more than half of code defects ([Kemerer & Paulk 2009](https://manuscript.isc.ac/Inventory/49/1241757.htm), abstract). Raising the inspection rate from 50 to 200 lines per hour cut detected faults from 1.6% to 0.6% of lines ([Basili & Perricone, via Panko & Ordway](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0804.0797), secondary). For code, the placeholder's k = 1,500 is therefore well matched. Its intercept is not: single reviewers catch a median of about 60% of errors, and individuals in lab inspections 22–66% ([Panko & Ordway](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0804.0797), secondary).

The owner, however, never reads code. The design shows it the agent's message, plans, and prose files, and prose gets skimmed hard. In Nielsen's instrumented-browser data, users read half the words only on pages of **111 words or fewer**, about 20% of an average 593-word page, and **18% of any words added** ([Nielsen 2008](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-little-do-users-read/), full text). A later study found **57% of viewing time above the fold and 74% in the first two screenfuls** ([NN/g scrolling study](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/), secondary). At the other extreme, the inspection optimum for documents is about one page per hour ([Gilb & Graham, via TU Munich slides](https://wwwbroy.in.tum.de/lehre/vorlesungen/sqm/ss10/slides/lecture04.pdf), secondary), while ordinary silent reading of non-fiction runs at **238 words per minute** ([Brysbaert 2019](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/linguistics/2019-brysbaert.pdf), abstract). An accountable owner reading an agent's plan at normal speed sits between web skimming (k ≈ 70–110 words) and code inspection (k ≈ 1,500). No study measures it directly, so k = 500 is a central guess, swept from 100 to 1,500. Nielsen's marginal read rate supplies the floor of 0.18. The probability of catching a contradiction is then attention times q = 0.8. That q sits between Baum's fitted ceilings (0.63–0.82) and the 86–90% detection of salient wrong items in Huang and Staub. The owner holds the domain knowledge for their own intent, which is what made incorrect-fact detection hard (15–43%) for Villamizar's novices.

| New words before the passage | 0 | 500 | 1,500 | 3,000 | 6,000 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Placeholder, 1/(1 + w/1,500) | 1.00 | .75 | .50 | .33 | .20 |
| Attention, k = 1,500 | 1.00 | .80 | .59 | .45 | .34 |
| Attention, k = 500 (central) | 1.00 | .59 | .39 | .30 | .24 |
| Attention, k = 100 | 1.00 | .32 | .23 | .21 | .19 |
| Kept contradiction, central (attention × 0.8) | .80 | .47 | .31 | .24 | .19 |

The placeholder row measures w cumulatively across the change. The other rows measure it as words before the passage within one stop, so the table compares shapes, not identical inputs.

The data favour position within a stop over words accumulated across stops. Baum found the first-versus-second large review effect "nearly non-existent": the swap defect was found in 9 of 25 first reviews and 10 of 25 second reviews. Vigilance research puts about half of the time-on-task decrement in the first 15 minutes ([Teichner, via Frontiers in Psychology 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.00237/full), secondary) and a 10–15% drop by 30 minutes ([Mackworth, via Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vigilance_(psychology)), secondary). NASA field data even found inspection meetings longer than two hours caught more defects, probably because longer documents got longer meetings ([Shull & Seaman 2008](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20090004415/downloads/20090004415.pdf), full text). Position, by contrast, is exactly what the scrolling data measure, and the harness can compute it because every correction quotes its passage. A position rule still produces a length effect: the average share of a document read falls as the document grows. The design measures w cumulatively so an arm cannot dodge the curve by splitting a long document across stops. But splitting a review into smaller sittings genuinely raises detection in every review dataset the research found, and the study already charges each extra stop to the owner as a touchpoint. The composite therefore uses position as the primary variable, adds a small session drag, and keeps the cumulative form as a sensitivity case. If the cumulative form stays primary, the write-up should present it as a choice about what to reward, not as a finding about attention.

## Rate salience once, blind, and freeze it with the seed

Salience can be assigned before any runs because it behaves like a property of the item, not of the person eliciting it. Across 14 quasi-experiments with 134 subjects, the same domain elements were reliably found or reliably missed regardless of the analyst's experience. In the simple domain, 47% of elements were found in more than half of sessions and 44% in fewer; in the complex one, 23% and 46% ([Aranda et al., visibility study](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.13691), full text). The authors trace visibility to an element's importance to the domain, how its relationships make the domain complex, and the interview script. Concepts were far more visible than activities in the complex domain: **50% of concepts against 6% of activities**. ACT-R motivates the rating question. Its base level tracks the log odds that an item will be needed, following Anderson and Schooler's analysis of how often the environment calls for a memory ([Pavlik & Anderson 2003](https://www.gwern.net/docs/www/act-r.psy.cmu.edu/f4b85d71f1bd93309c094119607097a071bb09e3.pdf), full text). So the rating should ask how often the owner's world calls for the item, not how important it feels.

The recommended rubric asks one question of each askable item: **how often would this owner, in their ordinary work, run into this concern?** High means weekly or more, typically a core object or rule of the product. Medium means occasionally, typically a rule that applies in some cases. Low means rarely: an edge case, a process step, an auxiliary mechanic, or a non-functional or style concern. Each low-tier category has support. Aranda's example of a low-importance element is means of payment in a shopping portal. ReqElicitGym's style requirements were elicited at a rate below 0.01 ([ReqElicitGym](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.18306), full text, tool-assisted; preprint). Security and privacy was the most common category analysts added after interviews, appearing for 53% of them ([Ferrari et al. 2022](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2208.00825), full text, tool-assisted). The Generative Agents importance prompt, a 1–10 scale anchored at "brushing teeth" and "a break up", is a reusable template once its anchors are reworded for requirements ([Park et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.03442v2), full text, tool-assisted). No study has checked that LLM-rated importance tracks human salience, though, so an LLM panel should be validated against human raters, not substituted for them unchecked.

The procedure follows from the study's own labelling standards. Three raters other than the maintainer rate every askable item from the oracle narrative and item text alone, blind to arms, policies, and results. Each item takes the median tier, and agreement should reach weighted kappa 0.7, the bar the design already sets for request-kind labels. An LLM panel can serve as a fourth rater: a model family different from the owner's, several samples, median taken. The tiers, the rubric, and the rater instructions go into the decisions log with a hash before counted runs. Any tier change after runs appears only as a labelled sensitivity case.

One distribution check guards the premise. Stated items are the ones the owner found visible, so the unsaid askable set should skew toward low and medium. If a third or more of it lands in the high tier, the oracle author has probably left unsaid things a real owner would have stated, and those items should be revisited before runs. The one-third threshold is a judgment call based on Aranda's visibility split. Salience in this sense is accessibility, which is different from stakes (how bad a violation would be). A high-stakes background assumption can be low-accessibility, which is the omitted "the" again. Stakes should not raise an item's recall tier.

## Scripted shifts should be mostly additions and clarifications

Changes in what the owner wants come in two streams that the simulator should keep apart. One is latent intent surfacing: items the owner always held, brought out by a cue. The other is genuine evolution: wants that did not exist until experience or outside events created them. Nurmuliani, Zowghi and Fowell's industrial case found both among three root causes: market demand, "developers' increased understanding of product domain", and organisational policy ([Nurmuliani et al. 2004](https://www.cse.chalmers.se/~feldt/courses/reqeng/papers/nurmulian_2004_analysis_of_req_volatility.pdf), full text). The recall rule generates the first stream with no scripting. The scripted emerging items and redirects carry the second. The classic simulator literature warns against leaving goals fixed: a goal-aware dialogue simulator did worse than a simpler one because "the assumption that the user goal stays fixed over the course of a dialogue is not correct" ([Schatzmann, Georgila & Young 2005](https://aclanthology.org/2005.sigdial-1.6.pdf), partial).

| Question | Evidence | Scripting guidance |
|---|---|---|
| How much change | Monthly creep of **1.0–3.5%** by software type; churn of existing requirements about twice creep in litigated projects; creep plus churn can top 30% of the original requirements within 18 months; more than 90% of projects change ([Jones 2000](https://totalmetrics.com/function-points/Function-Points-Litigation-Capers-Jones.pdf), full text; industry data, methods undisclosed). Overall volatility of 6% in a 16-month case (Nurmuliani) | Shifts touching roughly 5–30% of the oracle across the trajectory; the exact share is a judgment call that depends on how much calendar time ten changes stand for |
| What kind | **21 additions, 8 deletions, 8 modifications** among single-change requirement requests; modifications were mostly rewording for clarity (Nurmuliani) | Emerging items (additions) are the most common shift; clarification redirects outnumber true changes of mind; retirements are rare |
| When | Monthly volatility peaked at **16.85% against a 6% average** when specification reviews were completing (Nurmuliani); perfective, user-requested work is about 60% of maintenance effort ([Lientz, Swanson & Tompkins, via Schach](https://bjohnson.lmu.build/private/Schach_2003b.pdf), secondary; contested) | Latent surfacing should peak at the build; check that the recall rule produces this instead of scripting it. Spread emerging items over later changes, as use-driven needs |
| What content | Security and privacy the most common new category; new stakeholder roles for 22 of 30 analysts (Ferrari 2022); hidden goals only 4–8% of disclosures, with unknown unknowns coming from regulation, certification, and scenarios that contradict stated goals (Ferrari 2016) | Emerging items lean to non-functional concerns, new roles, and outside rules; core goals stay stable |
| Why | Three root causes, with no quantitative split recoverable (Nurmuliani) | Treat the understanding-versus-external mix as a sweep parameter, starting near even |

Jones's churn-over-creep ratio and Nurmuliani's additions-over-modifications count disagree about whether existing requirements or new ones change more. The litigated projects behind Jones's ratio are a selected sample, and Nurmuliani's modifications were mostly clarifications. So the guidance favours additions and treats clarification as the common form of modification. Delay also shifts the owner's stance in a direction worth modelling: the longer since intent formed, the more confident the owner and the less sensitive to omissions (Hernandez 2023). The dormancy sensitivity variant covers that.

Scripting every shift identically across arms buys comparability at a known cost: in reality, evolution partly depends on what the owner is shown. Boehm counted "I'll know it when I see it" expectations among the forces that unsettled airtight requirements ([Boehm 2000](https://dblp.org/rec/journals/computer/Boehm00a), abstract). Jones reports that prototypes cut later creep by 10–25%. A survey of over 300 managers and developers found some prototype types associated with higher later volatility ([Ferreira et al. 2011](https://mayoclinic.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/reducing-the-risk-of-requirements-volatility-findings-from-an-emp/), abstract). Requirements inspection lowered volatility while more user representatives raised it ([Zowghi & Nurmuliani 2002](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/bitstream/10453/2350/3/2004003534.pdf), full text). Showing more probably moves discovery earlier and also seeds new wants, and the fixed script removes both effects. A sensitivity variant can test whether conclusions depend on the script: let an emerging item fire at change k, or at the first stop within a short window before k whose text directly touches its topic, whichever comes first.

## Validate the owner's judgments and the conclusions' robustness, not one person's impression

The plan to calibrate by having the maintainer play the owner on a few trajectories cannot carry the weight placed on it. Which person played the client explained **18–26%** of elicitation effectiveness in one experiment, more than analyst experience, which explained almost none (R² = 0.02) ([Aranda et al., experience study](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.12538), full text). People are not even consistent with themselves: agents built from interviews were scored against each participant's own two-week test-retest agreement ([Park et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.10109), abstract). Estimating a catch probability near 0.4 to within ±0.1 at 95% confidence takes about **90 independent observations** (this report's binomial calculation), far more than a few trajectories supply. And the maintainer wrote the oracle and holds the hypotheses under test. One person's play-through is a useful smoke test, not a calibration.

Once the probabilities come from formulas, realism reduces to two questions. Are the owner model's per-event judgments right? And do the conclusions survive every plausible value of the constants? Five checks answer them without anyone playing the owner.

The first check labels the judgments. People label a sample of about 100 replayed stops for contradictions and touch levels, the same protocol and kappa 0.7 bar the design uses for request kinds. The diligent policy additionally needs high contradiction recall on fixed scenarios with planted contradictions. The likely failure is under-reporting: in a 451-person comparison, LLM simulators "quietly pivot rather than push back", and GPT-4o as the simulated user changed strategy after agent errors 19.1% of the time against humans' 8.4% ([Sim2Real gap study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11245), full text, tool-assisted; preprint).

The second check replays real human transcripts, following ReqElicitGym. Its authors fed 555 real interviewer turns from 33 elicitation sessions into their simulated stakeholder and compared each disclosure decision with the real stakeholder's: **kappa 0.73, premature disclosure 0.08, missed disclosure 0.13** ([ReqElicitGym](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.18306), full text, tool-assisted; preprint). The same corpus, with an oracle built from its annotated implicit requirements, would test this study's question gating and the targeted-versus-broad rule against real people. Whether the corpus is publicly available was not confirmed. It also tests interviews, not plan reviews, so it validates the question channel more than recall on review.

The third check replays the simulator's own logged stops whenever its instructions or model change, as the design already does for leaks. Replaying logged feedback ranked coding systems like live runs did, at Spearman 0.82–0.99 ([ConvCodeWorld](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.19852), abstract).

The fourth check uses stimuli modelled on published experiments. Plant the same contradiction in a short plan and a long one (Baum). Cue the same unsaid item directly in one plan and adjacently in another (Scullin). Run a story review with and without an explicit comparison list (Villamizar's 23% against 92%). The harness fixes the rates by construction, so these checks test whether the classification stage feeds it the right inputs, and whether the end-to-end rates land inside the published bands.

The fifth check sweeps the plausible box: k ∈ {100, 500, 1,500}, q ∈ {0.6, 0.8, 0.9}, tier spacing ±50%, λ ∈ {0, 1, 3}, dormancy on or off, and cumulative against per-stop w. Report the arm comparison across all of it. Conclusions that hold everywhere are robust, and any that flip are reported as depending on the parameters. The design already reports results as a function of catch probability, so this extends an existing commitment.

The existing audits stay: tau2-style annotation of simulator errors as critical or benign, and leak audits that now tell licensed recall lines apart from leaks. If human input becomes available later, many people each judging a few replayed stops ("would an owner holding this intent notice or recall this here?") average over individual differences. That is better than one person playing through whole trajectories.

## What rests on data and what rests on judgment

| Claim | Grade | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Detection falls as the amount reviewed grows, with a long tail rather than a cliff | Strong in direction | Baum (controlled); Kemerer & Paulk; Basili & Perricone; NASA found no natural threshold |
| A single reader's ceiling is well below 1 | Strong | Baum 0.63–0.82; lab inspections 22–66% |
| Hyperbolic shape with k ≈ 1,500 words for code | Moderate | Two controlled points; an exponential fits them equally well |
| Prose constants k = 500, floor 0.18 | Judgment | Bracketed by web skimming and code inspection; no data on owners reading plans |
| Position within a stop beats cumulative words | Moderate | Scroll data; Baum's null ordering effect; vigilance effects modest |
| Power or log forgetting over exponential | Strong | Rubin & Wenzel, 210 data sets |
| Cued recall far exceeds free recall; focal cues beat non-focal | Strong in direction | Aranda 35% to 71%; Scullin .73 against .18 |
| Recall table anchors (.25 and .75 at medium salience) | Moderate | Prospective-memory and omission-detection bands, transferred from lab intentions |
| Tier spacing, fan size, broad-question rule, dormancy factor | Judgment | Shaped by the cited directions, sized by choice |
| Silence goes unnoticed without an outside expectation | Strong | Omission neglect; Huang & Staub; Porter; Villamizar |
| Salience is a stable item property | Moderate | Aranda, consistent across analysts |
| Requirements change on most projects and peak at reviews | Moderate | Jones (industry data); one industrial case |
| LLM owners over-cooperate and leak under prompt-only control | Moderate | Sim2Real (preprint); tau2; HumanEvalComm |

The deepest limitation is transfer. Every constant beyond the curve shapes comes from a neighbouring task: vocabulary learning, laboratory intentions formed minutes earlier, web browsing by people with no stake in the page, code inspection, consumer ratings. None comes from owners reviewing plans for software they commissioned. The research found no study reporting what fraction of final requirements first surfaced at a prototype, demo, or plan review. So the "I'll know it when I see it" rate itself is unquantified. Agents also write long, polished, confident documents, and Hernandez, Han and Kardes found that diagnostic-looking information lowers sensitivity to what is missing. A simulator without that effect is likely biased toward arms that produce comprehensive-looking specifications, because real owners would catch fewer of those documents' silences. Two more effects stay unmodelled, each known only from abstracts. Retrieval-induced forgetting would make a group's remaining items harder to recall after some are asked about ([Appan & Browne 2010](https://aisel.aisnet.org/jais/vol11/iss5/2), abstract). The misinformation effect would have owners adopt an analyst's plausible wrong detail as their own memory, more so in conversation than in forms ([Appan & Browne 2012](https://misq.umn.edu/the-impact-of-analyst-induced-misinformation-on-the-requirements-elicitation-process.html), abstract). The second would make a missed contradiction sticky. The conditionalisation rule captures only part of that. Two key validation sources, the Sim2Real study and ReqElicitGym, are 2026 preprints. ACT-R defaults come from secondary notes because the reference manual was unreachable. And the owner model's own classification is a new source of error, which the label checks must bound before the formulas mean anything.

## Conclusion

Separating attention from memory turns a calibration problem that looked like it needed a human into a set of bounded, independently checkable parts. The policies become clean bounds on attention. Memory stays fallible under every policy, so the diligent owner cannot become a channel for leaking the oracle. And the remaining uncertainty sits in a handful of named constants that a sweep can expose, where one person's play-through would have hidden them. The composite also sharpens what the study can learn. Under realistic prose constants, a passage deep in a long plan is read at close to the 0.18 floor. Extra plan text then buys much less correction per word than the placeholder implied, and recall on review returns value only where text directly touches an unsaid item. The quantity that separates the arms becomes direct touches on unsaid intent per thousand words shown, not words shown.

The omission literature points to a lever the arms could compete on and the simulator must not erase. The largest gain in any study reviewed came from handing the reader an explicit list to check against: 23% to 92% of omissions caught. An arm that structures owner reviews as targeted questions about named concerns is doing in practice what that list did, and the targeted-versus-broad question rule is what lets the simulator credit it. Getting that rule right, and validating it against real human transcripts, matters more for the study's fairness than any decimal place in the attention curve.
