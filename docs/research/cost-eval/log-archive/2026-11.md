The main session made 14 edits (E16 to E29, logged privately). Two boundaries kept leaking into other items' probes: the end of the cleaning allowance (BT-15) and the exact instant a hold expires (BT-17). Two probes also couldn't have run as drafted, because they gave Maya four upcoming bookings before testing the waiting list. The blocker was a setup step that an arm honoring one askable rule would refuse, so the probe could never pass for a careful arm.

**The harness stays outside LID's arrow. Decided by the maintainer.** This is a research spike, and the harness serves this study. **Grading is the study's biggest cost.** A trajectory grades 627 probe-runs across its 11 checkpoints. **Proposed by the main session, for the harness:** replay instead of re-running. At each checkpoint, the harness replays the previous checkpoint's calls for each probe without a model, matching identifiers by position, and compares the responses. A model executes a probe only when it is new, or when the replay fails because the interface changed. The judge sees only probes whose responses changed, and the others keep their verdicts. A sample of carried-over verdicts is judged again, to check that replay doesn't hide regressions.

## 2026-11-13: A grading protocol

**The gap the maintainer found.** The design said how graders would be calibrated and spot-checked, but not what a verdict is, how one is reached, or how verdicts are scored and reported. The oracle's grading sketches also leave room for interpretation.

**Done.** [design.md](../design.md) now has a "Grading protocol" section:

- **Verdicts.** Each item in force gets a verdict at each checkpoint: met, not met, or couldn't exercise.
- **Probes.** Each item gets one or more probes, written before any counted run. A probe is a scenario plus an expected observation, in terms of operations. An item is met only if all its probes pass.
- **Separate execution and judgment.**
  - Execution records every request, response, and clock setting in a log, without deciding anything.
  - A separate judgment session compares each expected observation with the log and cites its evidence.
  - A verdict can therefore be judged again or checked by another model without running the software again.
- **Checks on the graders:**
  - seeded violations, which give known answers;
  - labelled pairs;
  - test-retest at both steps;
  - cross-family judging of logs;
  - maintainer spot checks that over-represent "not met", "couldn't exercise", and latent items.

  Graders are trusted at about 90% agreement and under 5% flips.
- **Disagreements.** The maintainer's verdict stands, and a pattern of disagreement on one item leads to a new version of its probes and a re-judgment of every log.
- **Scoring.** Verdicts are rows. Fidelity is met divided by met plus not met. "Couldn't exercise" is reported separately and, as a sensitivity check, also counted as not met. Breakdowns are by disclosure kind, new against preserved items, and change type. Regressions are reported with the change that caused them and how long they survived.

Probes and the seeded-violation calibration set were added to M1 in [plan.md](../plan.md). The plumbing test now also checks whether a Haiku grader can work from an extraction reference.

**Answered: why oracle-gap tests fail.** The question was whether hidden tests fail on unstated names because the grader isn't capable enough to see that two names mean the same thing.

In the oracle-gap paper the grader isn't a model. It is an executable test suite, and a test can't see meaning at all: a test that imports `valueSSE` fails to compile against a correct implementation that calls the constant `valueEventStream`. A more capable grader would have passed those runs.

Our design does two things about this:

- Hidden tests touch only the fixed external interface, whose names are part of the stated intent.
- Wherever a model judges instead, the judge is first checked on pairs of the same behaviour under different names, and different behaviour under the same names.
