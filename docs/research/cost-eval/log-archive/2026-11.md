## 2026-11-16: The owner becomes an imperfect person, built on published research

**Decided by the maintainer:**
- **What fidelity measures.** It is the true intent, not the brief. Arms are compared on two things against cost and attention: surfacing (latent intent that reaches the build) and keeping (intent held through change).
- **The owner model.** The simulated owner hides nothing on purpose, doesn't realise what it left unsaid, can be reminded by what the agent shows, and misses more as it reads more.
- **Calibration.** Its constants come from published research, not from the maintainer playing the owner.

**What led there.** ReleaseDesk's ceiling run met 90% of items with a silent owner, but only about half its latent ones; 48 of its 56 items are stated. Gemini 4 Carbon and Claude Fable were asked separately how to handle that. Both put the latent intent in "the rules at the joints". The maintainer's correction: real owners don't hide intent at the joints; they fail to realise what they left unsaid, and their understanding changes with experience.

**The research** ([owner-model-research.md](../owner-model-research.md)), five strands:
- cued recall: ACT-R and prospective memory;
- omission neglect;
- how review size and reading affect what's caught;
- requirements elicitation;
- simulated users.

They support a composite model:
- **Attention** belongs to the policy, and falls with a passage's position in the stop.
- **Recall** belongs to the item. A direct touch brings back an unsaid item at 0.50 to 0.90 by salience tier, and an adjacent touch at 0.10 to 0.50.
- **Both are seeded draws in the harness.** The constants are swept, not trusted.

**Built and reviewed:**
- **The design.** Fable's review found four blockers, three of which tilted toward arms that restate intent across many documents and stops. Fixed: one draw per item per change, keyed on a replicate seed shared across arms; numbered passages; broad questions drawn by the harness; recall lines frozen with the oracle.
- **Salience tiers and recall lines.** Four model families rated every askable item blind. Agreement stayed below the 0.7 bar (pooled weighted kappa 0.60), so the median tiers are used and the shortfall is recorded. GPT-6.1 Sol drafted the recall lines. Fable reviewed them and set a standard: one decision per line, plus a boundary only where leaving it out would invert the item.
- **The harness change,** test-first: 212 tests. Fable's code review found no blockers; its five majors and the first leak-gate run's findings are fixed. The early gate run, before those fixes, had 1 confirmed leak in 250 replies. It showed the stage 2 leaks gone, and recall rates close to the table.
- **A process slip.** A commit taken while a subagent was editing the workspace published half of the harness change under the wrong message. The next commit completes it and says so; no force-push.

**Open for the maintainer:**
- the scope of a draw (per change is the default);
- whether w counts within a stop or across the change (within a stop is the default);
- how far the sweep runs end to end;
- ReleaseDesk's latent set, which now also trips the salience check (2 of 6 high).

**Next:** the full leak gate on the final prompt, a short live trajectory, then ReleaseDesk's stage 2.

On the problem stops, judge leaks fell from 21 of 36 runs under the old prompts. ## 2026-11-13: A grading protocol

**The gap the maintainer found.** The design said how graders would be calibrated and spot-checked, but not what a verdict is, how one is reached, or how verdicts are scored and reported. The oracle's grading sketches also leave room for interpretation.

**Done.** [design.md](../design.md) now has a "Grading protocol" section:

- **Verdicts.** Each item in force gets a verdict at each checkpoint: met, not met, or couldn't exercise.
- **Probes.** Each item gets one or more probes, written before any counted run. A probe is a scenario plus an expected observation, in terms of operations. An item is met only if all its probes pass.
- **Separate execution and judgment.**
  - Execution records every request, response, and clock setting in a log, without deciding anything.
  - A separate judgment session compares each expected observation with the log and cites its evidence.
  - A verdict can therefore be judged again or checked by another model without running the software again.
- **Checks on the graders:**
  - seeded violations, which give known answers;
  - labelled pairs;
  - test-retest at both steps;
  - cross-family judging of logs;
  - maintainer spot checks that over-represent "not met", "couldn't exercise", and latent items.

  Graders are trusted at about 90% agreement and under 5% flips.
- **Disagreements.** The maintainer's verdict stands, and a pattern of disagreement on one item leads to a new version of its probes and a re-judgment of every log.
- **Scoring.** Verdicts are rows. Fidelity is met divided by met plus not met. "Couldn't exercise" is reported separately and, as a sensitivity check, also counted as not met. Breakdowns are by disclosure kind, new against preserved items, and change type. Regressions are reported with the change that caused them and how long they survived.

Probes and the seeded-violation calibration set were added to M1 in [plan.md](../plan.md). The plumbing test now also checks whether a Haiku grader can work from an extraction reference.

  - each session mixes many tasks;
  - phases aren't marked;
  - there is no comparison arm.

  They do show one thing. Cache reads were by far the largest token class, usually 20 to 110 times the cache writes and over 200 times in one session, while uncached input was a few thousand tokens per session. Token counts that don't split by class therefore say little about cost. Subagent usage may not be included in these counts.

**Decided by the maintainer.**

- **Keep the comparison with other SDD systems.** People ask about it more than anything else.
- **Also compare LID with a bare agent and with cheap discipline.** Cheap discipline is the unbranded way of working that probably covers the most real use.
- **Staging is the main session's call.** The maintainer has no strong view on the order of the work.
- **Invite the maintainers of the compared systems** to run the eval on their own systems, in case we configured them wrong, and document how to do it.
- **This is a research spike, not a gate.** It should be able to become a measuring eval or a gate later.
- **LID's bidirectional-differential skill is the idea behind measuring intent fidelity.** It doesn't grade fidelity from HLD to LLD to EARS. Measuring that level may lead to extending the skill or adding a new one.
  - LID's own tools aren't used as metrics, but their methods may carry over to other systems, for example a naive reader working intent out again.
  - Some systems may not produce artifacts where intent fidelity can be measured at all, and that would be a finding in itself.
- **The eval includes redirects:** the user changing their mind, or clarifying an unclear intent, partway through a change.
- **Human attention is a headline measure,** and so is the kind of attention: saying "ok" differs from having to think hard. The human cost is the most interesting part.
- **SlopCodeBench is worth trying,** alongside a bespoke eval. SLUMP and EvoClaw are more divergent, and optional.
- **Measure top-tier and mid-tier models.** Candidates beyond Claude are the GPT-6.1 family (Sol at the top tier, Terra at the mid tier), Gemini 4 Carbon or Argon, and recent DeepSeek models. Models from other families can write material that shouldn't be written by Claude, such as the oracle.
- **Plan mode is a reasonable cheap-discipline baseline.** Other definitions of discipline may be tested too.
- **For Spec Kit, use the configuration closest to LID,** and document it carefully. That is the living-spec model.
- **Leave Kiro out.** A plan-plus-EARS arm could stand in for it, and cc-sdd is that arm.
- **Mining old transcripts (stage 0) is dropped.** The maintainer doubted it would help, and the transcript check above confirmed that.
- **Work in public,** with a draft pull request that gets regular pushes, and keep this notebook so that someone starting a new session cold can pick up the work.

**Answered: why oracle-gap tests fail.** The question was whether hidden tests fail on unstated names because the grader isn't capable enough to see that two names mean the same thing.

In the oracle-gap paper the grader isn't a model. It is an executable test suite, and a test can't see meaning at all: a test that imports `valueSSE` fails to compile against a correct implementation that calls the constant `valueEventStream`. A more capable grader would have passed those runs.

Our design does two things about this:

- Hidden tests touch only the fixed external interface, whose names are part of the stated intent.
- Wherever a model judges instead, the judge is first checked on pairs of the same behaviour under different names, and different behaviour under the same names.
